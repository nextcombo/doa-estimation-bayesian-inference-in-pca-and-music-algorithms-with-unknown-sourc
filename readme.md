# [DOA Estimation] Bayesian Inference in PCA and MUSIC Algorithms with Unknown Source Numbers [Attached MATLAB Code]

The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a common method for projecting data onto low-dimensional uncorrelated components, but it does not specify the optimal number of components. Similarly, the Multiple Signal Classification (MUSIC) algorithm is an orthogonal MUSIC algorithm based on PCA for direction of arrival (DOA) estimation of sinusoidal sources, but it requires prior knowledge of the number of sources. Therefore, accurately estimating the number of sources is crucial for the performance of these algorithms. This paper demonstrates that for uncorrelated eigenvectors, both PCA and MUSIC can actually return accurate joint maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimates, but only approximate this MAP estimate in correlated cases. Subsequently, we use Bayesian methods to calculate the MAP estimate of source numbers in PCA and MUSIC algorithms for the first time. Intuitively, this MAP estimate corresponds to the highest probability of signal subspace projection noise variance still dominating when the signal plus noise variance is dominant. In simulations of overlapping multi-frequency sound sources in linear sensor arrays, our precise MAP estimate far outperforms the AIC—a commonly used method for estimating the number of components in PCA and MUSIC algorithms.
Introduction: In many array signal processing systems, such as radar, sonar, and antenna systems, linear sensor arrays are the most fundamental and general mathematical model. Since distant sources with different arrival directions (DOAs) will excite the sensor array at different angle frequencies, the output data from the array is the sum of sinusoidal signals [1]. Therefore, one common problem in array systems is detecting the number of sources from noisy sinusoidal signals and their frequencies and DOAs.
In literature, most studies only consider single-frequency or narrow-band sources (i.e., sources with non-overlapping frequencies), for which line spectrum estimation is a commonly used method (e.g., [2,3]). When there are few sources, the DOA line spectrum has sparsity, which can be effectively estimated using techniques such as atomic norms (also known as total variation norms) [1,2], Lasso [4,5], and Bayesian compression sampling [6,7] among others. An atomic norm method was also given near-optimal bounds in [8].
However, this paper focuses on a more general case —— arbitrary number of overlapping multi-frequency sources. In such cases, the most commonly used DOA and frequency estimation algorithms are Discrete Time Fourier Transform (DTFT), MUSIC, and ESPRIT, which originate from signal processing techniques [9]. Both MUSIC and ESPRIT belong to subspace methods, aiming to extract the signal subspace and noise subspace from noisy data space through feature decomposition. Although ESPRIT has lower computational complexity, MUSIC is more popular in practice [10,11]. For example, in intelligent antenna models, the MUSIC algorithm for DOA estimation is shown to be more stable and accurate than ESPRIT in smart antenna models [12,13]. Moreover, one of the main drawbacks of ESPRIT is the need for two translation-invariant subarrays to utilize the rotation-invariant subspace of angle frequencies [9].
Therefore, for this general case, this paper focuses on DTFT and MUSIC algorithms. Although these methods can achieve high resolution DOA estimation for uncorrelated and weakly correlated sources, it is necessary to know the number of sources K beforehand [9,14]. Estimating K accurately in practical applications is a key issue for these methods. The goal of this paper is to provide an optimal maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate for unknown K.
Related Work Since MUSIC is a variant of Principal Component Analysis (PCA), the most common method for estimating K is based on feature value methods in clustering literature (see [14]), where the most widely used is the information criterion, such as Minimum Description Length (MDL) [15] and PCA's Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [16]. Despite that, MDL and AIC are only asymptotic approximations of maximum likelihood (ML) estimation under infinite data [15,17]. Similarly, hard threshold schemes based on feature value methods are mostly heuristic [14], and only optimal in asymptotic scenarios [18].
Although PCA was proposed in the early 20th century [19,20], it remains one of the most popular data dimensionality reduction methods [21]. The paper indicates that PCA is equivalent to the maximum likelihood estimation of a factor analysis model with additive Gaussian white noise (AWGN). Besides heuristic methods based on eigenvalues, the non-asymptotic probability method for estimating K involves Bayesian theory. However, the posterior distribution of K and its components in PCA is often very complex and does not belong to any known distribution family [23,24]. Therefore, its closed-form solution remains an open problem in the literature [25,26,27]. For this reason, the MAP estimate of K in the literature can only be approximated by Laplace approximations, variational Bayes [28], and sampling-based Markov chain Monte Carlo [24,26,27] methods.
According to our knowledge, Theorem 5.1 in [25] may be the latest attempt to derive the exact MAP estimate for K in PCA. Unfortunately, this theorem imposes a standard normal - gamma prior form on the signal amplitude and noise variance, thus still involving two unknown hyperprior parameters. Before estimating K, [25] estimates these two unknown parameters using a heuristic plug-in method.
Our contribution differs from [25] as we use an uninformative prior without introducing any hyperprior parameters. To this end, we derive two new probability distributions, namely the double gamma distribution and the double inverse gamma distribution, in Appendix A. These new distributions will help us calculate the exact MAP estimate for K and the posterior mean estimate of the signal and noise variances without the need for PCA and MUSIC models or source prior knowledge. Intuitively, our MAP estimate \(\widehat{K}\) is equivalent to selecting the dimensionality of the signal subspace K such that the probability of higher signal-to-noise variance than that projected onto the subspace is highest.
These new distributions are essentially the natural forms of the joint posterior distributions of signal and noise variances in a PCA model. The motivation for introducing these new distributions is that under the assumption of Gaussian noise, both the posterior distributions of noise variance and signal-plus-noise variance follow inverse gamma distributions because they are conjugate to each other. Additionally, since the posterior of signal-plus-noise variance must dominate the noise variance, the dual inverse gamma distribution naturally emerges as the joint distribution under this dominance constraint. Therefore, these new distributions also apply to the joint estimation of unknown source and noise variances in general linear AWGN models.
With these new distributions, we will demonstrate that for both known and unknown noise variances, PCA and MUSIC can actually return joint MAP estimates of uncorrelated principal vectors / steering vectors. However, in the case of correlated principal vectors / steering vectors, these methods can only approximate this joint MAP estimate.

Conclusion This paper derives closed-form solutions for the MAP estimation of source number K in PCA, MUSIC, and DTFT spectral methods. To this end, we also derive two new probability distributions, namely the double gamma and the double inverse gamma distributions. With these distributions, we recognize that the posterior probability distribution of K considers all possible binomial combinations of signal and noise subspaces within the noisy data space. The MAP estimate for K corresponds to the dimensionality of the signal subspace, where the probability of noise variance dominating signal variance is highest.
In the simulation of linear sensor arrays, we also found that for accurate estimation, the signal-to-noise ratio should be higher than -10dB, meaning that the estimated noise percentage \(\bar{\tau}\) should be less than 90% (i.e., the projection noise bias on the signal space should be less than three times the source amplitude bias).
For overlapping multi-frequency sound sources, our MAP estimation method has been shown to outperform feature-based methods (such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) in PCA). However, our MAP estimation method is based solely on the average magnitude variance and uncorrelated principal vectors / steering vectors. Therefore, future research directions for MAP estimation based on individual magnitude and correlated principal vectors are interesting.
